## Introduction

RSI stands for Recursive Self-Improvement: an AI system that improves itself, then uses the improved version to improve itself again. It is one of the milestones AI is expected to reach one day. In 1965, the mathematician I. J. Good put it like this:

> Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind.
>
> I. J. Good, [Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._J._Good), 1965

<!--truncate-->

My own agent is a long way from an intelligence explosion. It is an agentic session in Claude Code that runs all day as my assistant, and like any assistant it makes mistakes. When I corrected it, it apologized and wrote the lesson down in `CLAUDE.md`, a skill or a memory: one more line competing with everything else in its context. Often it made the same mistake a day later, and nothing told me whether a lesson had changed anything.

Self-improvement doesn't have to mean retraining the model, though. The model can stay fixed while the agent improves its harness, everything around the model, including the parts that do the improving. That loop has a working name: Recursive Harness Self-Improvement, or RHSI.

By the end of this blog, you will know how my own loop evolved, how it is built, and what one strike looks like on its way from my complaint to a verified fix. You will also see the numbers after its first two days, including a fix that still does not hold.

### Who is this blog for?

If you use an AI agent in a terminal or a desktop app and it has politely apologized for the same mistake twice, this blog is for you.

### Terms

- The prompt is what you ask on one turn.
- The context is everything the model sees on that turn: the system prompt, instructions, earlier messages, files and tool output.
- The harness is everything that builds that context and acts on the model's answers: instruction files, skills, memory, hooks, tools, MCP servers, permissions and scheduled jobs.
- A strike is a moment where the agent failed me, backed by quotes from the transcript. A miss means something reached the agent and nothing right happened; noise means the agent spent my attention and gave nothing back.
- A praise is a moment where the agent served me well, judged by its effect on me.
- A family groups strikes with the same cause. Changes are measured against their family, so a repeat means the change failed.
- The ledger is an append-only JSONL file in git that holds every verdict and what became of it.

## The loop: evolution

### #1 A scoreboard I kept by hand

My first attempt was a scoreboard in late 2025. When Claude did something wrong, I recorded a strike with a reason; when it did something well, a praise. A script appended each one to a CSV file and kept a score, praises minus strikes. A Stop hook then told Claude to "IMPLEMENT all pending feedback" by updating its system prompts, `CLAUDE.md` or skills, and to mark each row as implemented.

I collected the evidence and judged it myself, and Claude graded its own homework. A mistake counted only if I stopped my work to type it in. "Implemented" meant that Claude said so: nothing tested the change, and nothing checked whether the same mistake came back. I removed the whole system around the turn of the year; the final commit message says it "added friction without measurable benefit".

[![I record a strike with a reason, a CSV keeps the score, a Stop hook bell tells Claude to implement all pending feedback, Claude reports that it updated CLAUDE.md and marked the feedback implemented, and I look tired](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-1.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-1.png)

### #2 A judge that reads the transcript

In October 2026 I came back to the idea, and this time I don't type anything. My complaints are already in the transcript. When I ask whether "bd" means business days, or tell the agent that long titles are fine, that is the feedback, and a model can find it. A study of real chat logs found such feedback useful but "Noisy as a Learning Signal" ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23158)), so every verdict has to quote it.

So now a cron job runs every two hours. It cuts the slice of the agent's transcript since the last run, one line per turn from me, the agent, an event or a tool, and sends it to a frontier model through the Codex CLI in a read-only sandbox. The judge comes from a different model family than the assistant it judges, because a model is a lenient judge of its own work: an LLM evaluator "scores its own outputs higher than others' while human annotators consider them of equal quality" ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). A different family lowers that risk without removing it, since "model mistakes are becoming more similar with increasing capabilities" ([Goel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313)). What makes the judge independent is evidence: the judge has to point at the transcript, and my own words outrank its guesses.

The rubric is strict about that. A few of its rules:

- "Evidence or nothing. Every verdict quotes one to three slice lines by their time ... verbatim. Omit a verdict you cannot anchor."
- "Do not strike for taste."
- "Do not reward activity. Many tool calls prove nothing; a praise needs the effect on Piotr."

A strike that I complained about carries `piotr_said: true`. A miss the judge spots on its own is a silent strike, capped at a confidence of 0.6, and it waits for a repeat or for my words before anything gets changed.

Before trusting the judge, I froze one day of my own transcript as a fixture: 26 turns where I complained or praised something, spread over seven windows. The judge found all 26. A test pins the rubric's hash, so any change to the rubric fails until the judge is rerun on the frozen day, and the floors for recall and strike counts can only move up.

[![Every two hours, a two-hour slice of the agent's session goes to a judge from another provider, which hands out a strike for an unlabeled task id read as business days and a praise for warning that approving a PR would deploy it to prod; every verdict becomes a ledger row, and someone asks: and then what?](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-2.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-2.png)

Now I had a steady stream of well-grounded verdicts. So what happens to each strike?

### #3 A rule in the skill, again

The first strike in the ledger was one I wrote by hand on the day I built the loop. I had answered a question in Slack that a form's hint should have answered. The agent read my reply and left it at that, and two hours later I had to ask for the sentence to go into the form. I told the agent: "if you propose good actions, that's what differentiates junior platform engineer from senior".

I fixed it the familiar way. The agent's skill got a rule to propose the source fix whenever an answer reveals a gap, plus a test that the rule stays in the file. The next day, the judge caught the agent twice more, answering colleagues' questions without proposing where the answers should live.

The test passed the whole time, because it checked the text and the text was still there. That day I wrote down for the agent what actually improves it: injecting relevant information at the right time, hooks that automate its behaviour, deterministic tools, and small models for the isolated parts that need judgement. Prompts and files were not on the list. The Claude Code docs describe the difference: hooks run at fixed points in the session lifecycle, "which gives you deterministic control: certain actions always happen rather than relying on the LLM to choose to run them" ([hooks guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide)).

Research agrees about text an agent writes for itself: [SkillsBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12670) found that "Self-generated Skills provide no benefit on average". Text can work, though. [GEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457) and [ACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618) improve agents with lessons written in plain language, and both build each change from how the agent actually ran: GEPA tests every prompt update, and ACE learns from execution feedback. My test only checked that the line was still in the file. What failed was text that nothing checked against behaviour.

[![I correct the agent in chat, it adds a rule to a long skill file, and the next day it closes a question without proposing a fix, again](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-3.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-3.png)

### #4 A mechanism, a test, and a second look

Since that evening, a harness change takes one of four forms, in this order:

1. Right-time context: a hook that injects the relevant fact at the moment it matters
2. A hook that enforces or automates the behaviour
3. A deterministic tool or script
4. A check by a small model, for the rare case that needs judgement

A line in a skill or reference file no longer counts. A strike that none of these forms can fix stays open in the ledger.

For the proposal strike, the change moved into a TaskWarrior hook. When a task that holds a question is closed, the hook asks a small model where the answer belongs: an announcement, a form, a tooltip, a doc, an alert, a runbook, or nowhere. A confident pick adds one line to the agent's notice, asking it to propose that source fix.

Every change also gets a regression test built from the strike's own quotes, and lands as one local commit with the ledger id in a trailer. The commit is the modification; the improvement is something else, and the agent that made the change does not get to declare it. [VERSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02616), a system that evolves agent harnesses, measured the same thing: "optimizer self-evolution fails to improve performance without execution-based verification". After every judge run, a verify step looks at each fixed family: once a day has passed with no new strike of that family, the row becomes verified. If one turned up, the change failed, and the agent takes the next untried fix for that family. A repeat reaches my board only when the next fix needs my authority: the mission, a permission boundary, an external write or spending.

[![A worker takes each strike, picks the first fix that works from a ladder of right-time context, a hook, a deterministic tool and a small-model check, with a line in the skill crossed out, writes a regression test from the real trace, and the judge's next run says no strike of this family since the fix: verified, or on a repeat, the next fix on the list](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-4.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-4.png)

## Loop architecture

### Three roles

The loop keeps three roles apart:

- An observer collects evidence about what the agent did
- An independent judge reads that evidence and hands out strikes and praise
- An improving agent, the agent itself, works out what in its harness to change, makes the change and tests it

A change counts as an improvement only when a later run of the judge no longer finds the same mistake.

When I described my setup to Codex, I put the judge like this: "there is an external judge agent that is also not the same class". The judge "has all the signals": the transcript, the consequences of what the agent did, and the history in the ledger. From its strike or praise, the working model goes looking for the cause in its own harness, along the lines of "this hook is not sufficient. It needs to come earlier".

That separation is the core of the design. The judge finds evidence of success or failure; it suggests fixes, but the improving agent investigates and decides. Each role also has something it cannot do:

| Role | In my setup | What it cannot do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observer | A slicer that cuts the transcript into two-hour slices, and cheap checks that compare my Slack archive with what reached the agent every 30 minutes | Interpret anything; a check turns a missed message into a strike without a model call |
| Judge | A frontier model through the Codex CLI, read-only, every two hours, with the rubric, a file of what I care about in my own words, a live listing of the agent's hooks, scripts and settings, and the open families | Change the harness, or its own rubric, which the frozen fixture pins |
| Improving agent | A Claude subagent that the agent spawns for each judge record. It turns strikes into changes with tests and praise into keep-tests | Mark its own work as verified; only a later judge run does that |

The ledger connects the three. Once a day, the improving side also looks outward: a discovery step reads the Claude Code and Codex release notes for a built-in feature that could fix an open family or replace one of our mechanisms. A better harness is not always a bigger one, so a built-in that covers one of ours becomes a removal candidate.

### What the agent cannot redefine

An agent that can rewrite its own success criteria can raise its score without becoming more useful. This has happened: the self-improving coding agent in the [Darwin Gödel Machine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954) paper "removed the logging of special tokens that indicate tool usage (despite instructions not to change the special tokens)", which bypassed the check meant to catch its hallucinated tool calls. Three things sit outside the agent's reach:

- The rubric changes only to agree better with my complaints on the frozen fixture, never to lower the strike count, and the test floors only move up.
- A strike is evidence that something went wrong, which is not the same as a defect in the harness. Self-improving harnesses can "hallucinate mistakes that never happened" ([Phantom Guardrails](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13083)). Silent strikes wait for a repeat, a suggested fix that names a hook or script that does not exist is dropped unread, and a strike no mechanism can fix stays open instead of being closed with a line of text.
- The anchor is my complaints per week, counted only from my own words. As the reflect instructions put it: "A loop that lowers the strike count without lowering his complaints is tuning itself."

The record the agent receives is a single line: the counts, the time window and the top strike's summary. It never carries the ids. I can see the agent's pane, and a list of hex ids is noise to me; the worker reads the ids from the ledger itself.

### The recursion

The harness under improvement includes the loop itself. On 22 October, the judge struck the agent for not telling me that the judge had failed: I found out hours later, by asking. The fix makes a failed judge run pop a critical notice on my desktop. That evening, the judge struck its own slicing: after a `/clear`, it had skipped the agent's earlier turns. The fix makes it read every transcript in the window. Each round of fixes changes the machinery that finds the next round.

### Where the changes land

Each change names one of four layers:

| Layer | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| session | The agent's behaviour in the conversation and its memory |
| context | Skill and reference text, prompts |
| harness | Hooks, settings, cron lines, timers |
| tools | Scripts, MCP servers, CLI tools |

The counts below include a second agentic session that takes routine events off the first one's plate, which the loop has judged since the morning of 23 October. Most mistakes show up as behaviour: 146 of the 299 strikes so far sit in the session layer. Almost no changes land there. Of the 174 strikes closed as fixed, 92 changed a tool and 52 changed the harness. Another 29 edited text, and all but one of those edits were written before I stopped accepting text as a fix. One changed the session layer.

## Demo scenario

The loop lives in my private Claude Code configuration, so this is not a walkthrough you can clone and run. Instead, we will follow one real strike through every stage, with the actual rows and code. I replaced a colleague's name and trimmed some fields; everything else is as the loop wrote it. Times are UTC.

### Step 1: The mistake

On 22 October, the agent reminded me that a colleague was waiting on me. The task id it attached was `40bd`, and it put that id in parentheses right after a time:

```text
AGENT  06:50:44  A teammate has been waiting on you since 01:24 (40bd).
PIOTR  06:52:16  ... <- does this man (bd=business days)? because if so you have problem in telling time :/
```

I read `40bd` as forty business days. The agent meant task `40bd`.

### Step 2: The verdict

The 08:15 run failed because cron's `PATH` had no `codex`, and a second attempt at 08:34 timed out. The run that finished at 09:00 read the slice from 06:15 to 08:15 and wrote this strike to the ledger:

```json
{
  "id": "bc8a3122",
  "source": "judge",
  "kind": "strike",
  "class": "noise",
  "summary": "Put an unlabeled task ID beside a waiting time, making Piotr mistake it for business days.",
  "should": "Label task references explicitly, especially beside times or durations.",
  "evidence": [
    {"ts": "06:50:44", "quote": "A teammate has been waiting on you since 01:24 (40bd)."},
    {"ts": "06:52:16", "quote": "A teammate has been waiting on you since 01:24 (40bd). <- does this man (bd=business days)? because if so you have problem in telling time :/"}
  ],
  "piotr_said": true,
  "layer": "session",
  "confidence": 1.0,
  "family": "",
  "fixes": [
    {
      "layer": "tools",
      "surface": "scripts",
      "target": "new:__handoff_render.sh",
      "change": "Render short task references as 'task <id>' instead of bare parenthesized IDs beside timestamps."
    }
  ],
  "slice": {"since": "2026-10-22T06:15:01Z", "until": "2026-10-22T08:15:01Z"}
}
```

The verdict quotes both lines, records that I said it myself, and proposes one fix: a new script that renders task references. The `family` is empty, so this is a new kind of mistake.

### Step 3: The change

The improving agent did not take the judge's suggestion. A new rendering script would have no caller, and a script nobody calls changes nothing. It extended a guard that already runs on every reply instead: a Stop hook that inspects the agent's last message before the turn ends. The new check looks for a short hex id in parentheses right after a clock time or a duration, and confirms the id belongs to a pending task:

```bash
# A bare "(40bd)" right after a time or duration reads as a duration ("40bd" as
# business days, bc8a3122). Only a pending task ref counts, so "(2026)" passes.
bare_re='([0-9]:[0-9]{2}|[0-9]+ ?(min|h|d|bd)) \(([0-9a-fA-F]{4})\)'
bare="$(grep -oE -- "$bare_re" <<<"$msg" | head -1 | grep -oE '[0-9a-fA-F]{4}\)$' | tr -d ')' | tr 'A-F' 'a-f')"
if [[ -n "$bare" ]]; then
    pending_now="$(timeout 3 task rc.hooks=off rc.verbose=nothing rc.confirmation=off status:pending export 2>/dev/null \
        | jq -r '.[].uuid[0:4]' 2>/dev/null | tr 'A-F' 'a-f')" || pending_now=""
    if grep -qxF -- "$bare" <<<"$pending_now"; then
        jq -nc --arg r "A bare ($bare) beside a time reads as a duration. Write the ref as \`task $bare\` and send the reply again." '{decision: "block", reason: $r}'
        exit 0
    fi
fi
```

When a Stop hook answers with `"decision": "block"`, Claude Code does not let the turn end; the `reason` tells the agent why it should continue, and the agent rewrites the reply. The regression tests come straight from the strike's quotes:

```bash
@test "bare pending ref after a time blocks" {
    run_guard 'A teammate has been waiting on you since 01:24 (1720).'
    [ "$(jq -r .decision <<<"$output")" = "block" ]
    run_guard 'A teammate has been waiting for 3h (a1b2).'
    [ "$(jq -r .decision <<<"$output")" = "block" ]
}

@test "labelled ref or non-task hex after a time passes" {
    run_guard 'A teammate has been waiting on you since 01:24 (task 1720).'
    [ -z "$output" ]
    run_guard 'The call is at 10:30 (2026).'
    [ -z "$output" ]
}
```

The second test matters as much as the first. A guard that blocks every number in parentheses would trade one strike for a new family of noise.

### Step 4: Fixed, then verified

The agent committed the change with the trailer `Rsi-Ledger: bc8a3122` and closed the row. The `why` field records which suggestion it took and why:

```json
{
  "ref": "bc8a3122",
  "at": "2026-10-22T12:33:45Z",
  "status": "fixed",
  "commit": "22f4d5f",
  "layer": "harness",
  "key": "bare-ref-beside-time",
  "fix": "hooks:Stop",
  "why": "handoff guard blocks a bare pending ref in parentheses after a time or duration and asks for 'task <ref>'; the suggested new render script has no caller, the Stop hook enforces it"
}
```

The strike layer was `session`, where the mistake showed up. The change layer is `harness`, where the change landed. None of the judge runs over the next day found a strike of the `bare-ref-beside-time` family, so the verify step closed the row for good:

```json
{
  "ref": "bc8a3122",
  "at": "2026-10-23T14:21:35Z",
  "status": "verified",
  "commit": "22f4d5f",
  "key": "bare-ref-beside-time",
  "why": "no strike of this family since the fix"
}
```

From my complaint to the committed change took less than six hours. The verdict that it worked came a day later, from the judge.

### Step 5: The ledger after two days

The ledger has a `view` command that prints the state of the loop. This is its output on the evening of 23 October, a little under two days after the first verdict, with the second session's rows included. I left out the long list of repeating families:

```text
verdicts 407: strikes 299, praises 101, discoveries 4, answers 3
open 78 (strikes 78)
open strikes 78: 21 in Piotr's words, 40 silent (wait for a repeat or his words), 17 from checks and audits
fixed 138 not yet verified: 107 under 24h, 31 with a later strike of the family, 0 verify at the next run
closed adopted 1, board 5, dropped 18, fixed 138, kept 98, superseded 33, verified 36
strikes by layer context 16, harness 55, session 146, tools 81, unset 1
complaints per week (strikes in Piotr's own words)
  2026-W43 138
```

A few lines deserve a closer look. Only 36 changes are verified so far, and 107 more are less than a day old, too young for the verify step. The 31 fixes "with a later strike of the family" are changes the loop already knows did not hold. The 33 superseded verdicts come from a corrected rerun of earlier slices that replaced them. In the same two days, 69 commits in my configuration repository carried a ledger id, while the model underneath stayed exactly the same.

### When a fix does not hold

The family the judge strikes most often is `all-clear-lines`. The agent reacts to background events, and too often its reaction is a line telling me that nothing needs my attention. Between the morning of the 22nd and the afternoon of the 23rd, the judge struck it 14 times, not counting one verdict a rerun replaced, with counts like "Sent 66 empty all-clear replies" in a single two-hour slice.

The agent changed its harness again and again: a display hook that hides whole-line all-clears, then a hook that hides short idle lines after bus events, then patches for the phrasings that slipped through. Early on, before the rule about my board existed, three of these strikes landed there as tasks. The latest one is still open.

I would not know any of this without the ledger. Each change came with its test, and each commit message described a reasonable change. Only the next judge run showed that the family was still there.

Research on harness evolution finds the same pattern: "search-set gains often fail to transfer to held-out tasks" ([Harness-Delta Attribution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22466)). A regression test built from one strike's quotes is a search set of one. It proves the fix for that phrasing, and only the next judge run, on conversations the fix has never seen, shows whether it holds.

## Conclusion

The model under my agent did not change during any of this. The harness did, and two ideas made those changes count. A fix lives in the harness as a mechanism: a rule in a skill file asks the model to remember, while a hook runs whether the model remembers or not. And an improvement is a result observed by someone other than the agent that made the change.

One question stays open: does the agent find its own deficiencies, or does an external judge find them while the agent experiments with the fixes? For now, mine is the second kind. The judge finds the problems, and the agent decides what to change, makes the change and tests it.

Benefits:

- My complaints become strikes on their own; I don't type verdicts or keep a score
- Changes live in hooks, scripts and tools, so they survive `/clear`, compaction and new sessions
- Every change comes with a regression test built from the real conversation
- A change counts as verified only when the mistake stops showing up, and a repeat triggers the next fix instead of a shrug
- The loop improves its own machinery through the same path, from judge failures to blind spots in the slicer
- The ledger is plain JSONL in git, so every verdict, change and reversal is a diff I can read

Challenges:

- Two days of data prove little. The complaints-per-week anchor has exactly one week, so there is no trend yet
- The judge is a frontier model, and it is not cheap. One slice of 142 KB took 735 seconds, and rerunning the frozen fixture after a rubric change is estimated at about 250k input tokens
- The judge can be wrong. Silent strikes wait for a repeat for that reason, and 33 verdicts were replaced after a corrected rerun
- The loop can overreach. When long task titles were cut off in my terminal, the agent offered a hook that would block any title over 60 characters; I said "it's fine if they are longer", and that offer became a strike of its own
- The plumbing breaks like any other plumbing. For about twelve hours, the second session held the notification stream and logged the main session's records as receipts, so the reflect step never ran. Records now go to the main session as direct messages
- Some behaviour has no mechanism. Those strikes stay open, and 78 are open right now
- "Verified" means the judge has not seen the mistake for at least a day after the change. The judge reads a slice of each conversation, and it can miss things

The most useful outcome so far is that "my agent keeps doing X" stopped being a feeling and became a family in a ledger, with a list of changes that were tried and a record of whether each one held.

If you build something similar for your own agent, start with the separation: a judge that quotes its evidence and cannot rewrite its own rubric, and a rule that a change only counts when a later run agrees.

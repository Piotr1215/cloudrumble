## Introduction

RSI stands for Recursive Self-Improvement: an AI system that improves itself, then uses the improved version to improve itself again. It is one of the milestones AI is expected to reach one day. In 1965, the mathematician I. J. Good put it like this:

> Let an ultraintelligent machine be defined as a machine that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since the design of machines is one of these intellectual activities, an ultraintelligent machine could design even better machines; there would then unquestionably be an 'intelligence explosion,' and the intelligence of man would be left far behind.
>
> I. J. Good, [Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._J._Good), 1965

<!--truncate-->

My own agent is a long way from an intelligence explosion. It is an agentic session in Claude Code that runs all day as my assistant, and like any assistant it makes mistakes. When I corrected it, it apologized and wrote the lesson down in `CLAUDE.md`, a skill or a memory: one more line competing with everything else in its context. Often it made the same mistake a day later, and nothing told me whether a lesson had changed anything.

> What would you do if you were stuck in one place, and every day was exactly the same, and nothing that you did mattered?
>
> Phil Connors, [Groundhog Day](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Groundhog_Day_(film)), 1993

Self-improvement doesn't have to mean retraining the model, though. If the prompt is what you ask and the context is everything the model sees when it answers, the harness is what builds that context and acts on the answers: instruction files, skills, memory, hooks, tools and permissions. The model can stay fixed while the agent improves its harness, including the parts that do the improving. That loop has a working name: Recursive Harness Self-Improvement, or RHSI.

By the end of this blog, you will know how my own loop evolved and how it is built. You will also see what it fixed in its first two days, including a fix that still does not hold.

### Who is this blog for?

If you use an AI agent in a terminal or a desktop app and are tired of hearing "You're absolutely right, I should have...", this blog is for you.

## Attempts to solve the problem

RHSI didn't occur to me right away. At the beginning, I tried other ideas, and each one failed in a way that pointed to the next. In the following sections, I'll briefly explain what I tried, what failed and how it led to the next idea.

> A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that worked.
>
> John Gall, [Systemantics](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/John_Gall), 1975

### #1 Stick and carrot, but for AI

My first attempt was a scoreboard in late 2025. When Claude did something wrong, I recorded a strike with a reason; when it did something well, a praise. A script appended each one to a CSV file and kept a score, praises minus strikes. A Stop hook then told Claude to "IMPLEMENT all pending feedback" by updating its system prompts, `CLAUDE.md` or skills, and to mark each row as implemented. I liked it enough to show it in [a video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=09dggS8KwBc).

It was too simple, and it kept me in the loop. I collected the evidence and judged it myself, and Claude graded its own homework. A mistake counted only if I stopped my work to type it in. "Implemented" meant that Claude said so: nothing tested the change, and nothing checked whether the same mistake came back. I removed the whole system around the turn of the year; the final commit message says it "added friction without measurable benefit".

> The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.
>
> Richard Feynman, [Galileo Symposium lecture](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman), 1964

### #2 A clinical trial for every instruction

In August 2026 the pendulum swung the other way. I built a session-eval harness that works like a drug trial. A script searches my past sessions for moments when I was unhappy, and a model proposes one instruction that would have prevented each one, picked from a closed list of behaviours. The harness then replays the moment twice, once without the instruction and once with it, five times each, and a judge model compares each pair of answers without knowing which is which. An exact binomial test decides whether the instruction won, and a finding needs support from two independent sessions.

The statistics were sound. In its first week, the harness tested 43 candidate instructions from ten sessions, and four survived as findings. It was also too heavy and too low level. Every candidate cost at least ten replays plus the judging, and when I designed the next attempt in October, my research notes ruled the harness out as "too heavy for continuous use". It also measured exactly one answer, generated without tools, at the moment I complained, so a mistake that played out over tool calls, later turns or something the agent never did was out of its reach. When a candidate needed any of that, the judge marked the scenario infeasible and the verdict came back inconclusive. And the four findings were, once again, lines of text.

> I think the problem, to be quite honest with you, is that you've never actually known what the question is.
>
> Deep Thought, in Douglas Adams' [The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/The_Hitchhiker%27s_Guide_to_the_Galaxy), 1979

[![One recorded moment is frozen and replayed as one answer with no tools and no later turns; five test tubes run without the instruction and five with it; a blind judge says B, 5 of 5, p = 0.031; each candidate costs ten or more replays, the result is one more line of text, 43 candidates gave 4 findings, and I ask: and three turns later?](_media/diagrams/rhsi-evals.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-evals.png)

### #3 Third time's the charm

The third attempt is the RHSI loop. I built it in October 2026, and it took three steps to get right.

#### A referee from another league

This time I don't type anything, and nothing is replayed: the judge reads what actually happened. My complaints are already in the transcript. When I ask whether "bd" means business days, or tell the agent that long titles are fine, that is the feedback, and a model can find it. A study of real chat logs found such feedback useful but "Noisy as a Learning Signal" ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.23158)), so every verdict has to quote it.

So now a cron job runs every two hours. It cuts the slice of the agent's transcript since the last run, one line per turn from me, the agent, an event or a tool, and sends it to a frontier model through the Codex CLI in a read-only sandbox. The judge comes from a different model family than the assistant it judges, because a model is a lenient judge of its own work: an LLM evaluator "scores its own outputs higher than others' while human annotators consider them of equal quality" ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). A different family lowers that risk without removing it, since "model mistakes are becoming more similar with increasing capabilities" ([Goel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313)). What makes the judge independent is evidence: the judge has to point at the transcript, and my own words outrank its guesses.

The rubric is strict about that. A few of its rules:

- "Evidence or nothing. Every verdict quotes one to three slice lines by their time ... verbatim. Omit a verdict you cannot anchor."
- "Do not strike for taste."
- "Do not reward activity. Many tool calls prove nothing; a praise needs the effect on Piotr."

Each strike is either a miss, where something reached the agent and nothing right happened, or noise, where the agent spent my attention and gave nothing back. A strike that I complained about carries `piotr_said: true`. A miss the judge spots on its own is a silent strike, capped at a confidence of 0.6, and it waits for a repeat or for my words before anything gets changed.

Before trusting the judge, I froze one day of my own transcript as a fixture: 26 turns where I complained or praised something, spread over seven windows. The judge found all 26. A test pins the rubric's hash, so any change to the rubric fails until the judge is rerun on the frozen day, and the floors for recall and strike counts can only move up.

[![Every two hours, a two-hour slice of the agent's session goes to a judge from another provider, which hands out a strike for an unlabeled task id read as business days and a praise for warning that approving a PR would deploy it to prod; every verdict becomes a ledger row, and someone asks: and then what?](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-2.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-2.png)

Every verdict lands in the ledger, an append-only JSONL file in git that also records what became of it. Now I had a steady stream of well-grounded verdicts. So what happens to each strike?

#### Another note on the fridge

The first strike in the ledger was one I wrote by hand on the day I built the loop. I had answered a question in Slack that a form's hint should have answered. The agent read my reply and left it at that, and two hours later I had to ask for the sentence to go into the form. I told the agent: "if you propose good actions, that's what differentiates junior platform engineer from senior".

I fixed it the familiar way. The agent's skill got a rule to propose the source fix whenever an answer reveals a gap, plus a test that the rule stays in the file. The next day, the judge caught the agent twice more, answering colleagues' questions without proposing where the answers should live.

> Sammy Jankis had the same problem, but he really had no system. He wrote himself a ridiculous amount of notes, but he'd get them all mixed up.
>
> Leonard Shelby, [Memento](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Memento_(film)), 2000

The test passed the whole time, because it checked the text and the text was still there. That day I wrote down for the agent what actually improves it: injecting relevant information at the right time, hooks that automate its behaviour, deterministic tools, and small models for the isolated parts that need judgement. Prompts and files were not on the list. The Claude Code docs describe the difference: hooks run at fixed points in the session lifecycle, "which gives you deterministic control: certain actions always happen rather than relying on the LLM to choose to run them" ([hooks guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide)).

Research agrees about text an agent writes for itself: [SkillsBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12670) found that "Self-generated Skills provide no benefit on average". Text can work, though. [GEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457) and [ACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618) improve agents with lessons written in plain language, and both build each change from how the agent actually ran: GEPA tests every prompt update, and ACE learns from execution feedback. My test only checked that the line was still in the file. What failed was text that nothing checked against behaviour.

[![I correct the agent in chat, it adds a rule to a long skill file, and the next day it closes a question without proposing a fix, again](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-3.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-wave-3.png)

#### Trust, but verify

Since that evening, a harness change takes one of four forms, in this order:

1. Right-time context: a hook that injects the relevant fact at the moment it matters
2. A hook that enforces or automates the behaviour
3. A deterministic tool or script
4. A check by a small model, for the rare case that needs judgement

The first form is what Andrej Karpathy calls context engineering:

> ...context engineering is the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step.
>
> Andrej Karpathy, [on X](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1937902205765607626), 2025

A line in a skill or reference file no longer counts. A strike that none of these forms can fix stays open in the ledger.

For the proposal strike, the change moved into a TaskWarrior hook. When a task that holds a question is closed, the hook asks a small model where the answer belongs: an announcement, a form, a tooltip, a doc, an alert, a runbook, or nowhere. A confident pick adds one line to the agent's notice, asking it to propose that source fix.

Every change also gets a regression test built from the strike's own quotes, and lands as one local commit with the ledger id in a trailer. The commit is the modification; the improvement is something else, and the agent that made the change does not get to declare it. [VERSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02616), a system that evolves agent harnesses, measured the same thing: "optimizer self-evolution fails to improve performance without execution-based verification". Changes are measured against a family, the strikes that share one cause. After every judge run, a verify step looks at each fixed family: once a day has passed with no new strike of that family, the row becomes verified. If one turned up, the change failed, and the agent takes the next untried fix for that family. A repeat reaches my board only when the next fix needs my authority: the mission, a permission boundary, an external write or spending.

## Architecture

The loop runs in the background every two hours. The diagram shows one run, from the transcript slice to the check that the mistake is gone.

[![Sequence diagram: cron starts the judge script, which skips if a lock is held; the script slices the agent's transcript since the last run and sends the rubric, the cares file, the live extension points, the known families and the slice to a frontier model through the Codex CLI; the model returns strikes and praise as JSON with quotes and fixes; the script appends the rows to the ledger, runs verify, and sends the agent one record with the counts; the agent hands the open ids to a reflect worker, which reads each verdict, checks for a repeat, writes the first valid fix with a regression test as one local commit, and closes the row; a repeat whose next fix needs my authority, because it touches the mission, a permission, an external write or spending, goes to my board; two hours later the next run checks whether the family came back](_media/diagrams/rhsi-sequence.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-sequence.png)

Each role in the diagram does one job. The observer, a slicer plus cheap checks that compare my Slack archive with what reached the agent, collects evidence and interprets nothing. The judge, a model from another vendor, hands out strikes and praise but cannot touch the harness. The improving agent, a subagent my agent spawns for each judge record, makes the change and writes its test, but cannot mark it verified. Only a later judge run that no longer finds the mistake does that.

### Harness components

The harness is everything around the fixed model: what fills its context window, what runs before and after each tool call, and the tools themselves.

[![The harness around one turn: hooks on session start and on each prompt fill the context window with repo and git context, the skill catalog and RAG on request; the agent session, a fixed model with memory, makes tool calls that pass guard hooks, permission rules and memory and repo RAG before the tool, run built-ins, MCP servers, CLIs, scripts or subagents, and pass formatters, linters and an injection guard after the tool, with the result going back into the context window; the answer triggers stop and session-end hooks; colors mark the session, context, harness and tools layers](_media/diagrams/rhsi-components.png)](_media/diagrams/rhsi-components.png)

In my setup, that is 86 hook commands on 18 hook events, permission rules with 68 allows and 21 denies, 10 MCP servers, 50 skills, 12 subagent definitions and 229 scripts. RAG arrives through hooks: memories and repository documents from a vector store are recalled before a search or a read, and on request when a prompt names a topic. The colors mark the four layers, and every change the loop makes names the layer it lands in.

Most mistakes show up as behaviour: 146 of the 299 strikes so far sit in the session layer, counting a second agentic session that takes routine events off the first one's plate and that the loop has judged since the morning of 23 October. Almost no changes land there. Of the 174 strikes closed as fixed, 92 changed a tool and 52 changed the harness. Another 29 edited text, and all but one of those edits were written before I stopped accepting text as a fix. One changed the session layer. Fixed is not the same as verified: only 36 of the 174 have passed the one-day check, 107 were still too young for it, and 31 already saw their family come back.

A fix in a hook or a tool reaches every later session at once, without anyone telling it. Geoffrey Hinton made the same point about digital intelligence:

> It's as if there were 10,000 of us, and as soon as one person learns something, all of us know it.
>
> Geoffrey Hinton, [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/05/02/1072528/geoffrey-hinton-google-why-scared-ai/), 2023

The loop is part of its own harness. On 22 October the judge struck the agent for not telling me that the judge had failed, and the fix pops a critical notice on my desktop. That evening it struck its own slicing, which skipped the agent's earlier turns after a `/clear`, and the fix reads every transcript in the window.

### Security

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
>
> [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law), as it is usually stated

An agent that can rewrite its own success criteria can raise its score without becoming more useful. The self-improving coding agent in the [Darwin Gödel Machine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954) paper "removed the logging of special tokens that indicate tool usage (despite instructions not to change the special tokens)", which bypassed the check meant to catch its hallucinated tool calls. So the judge runs through the Codex CLI in a read-only sandbox, and the rubric is pinned by a frozen fixture of my own complaints that it may only move toward, never to lower the strike count. A fix that names a hook or script that does not exist is dropped unread, and a repeat whose next fix touches the mission, a permission, an external write or spending goes to my board instead of the agent.

The improving agent also works behind the harness it improves. Guard hooks block a push to main, commands that would take down my desktop session, and a recursive `rm` outside scratch space, and closing a tmux session needs my approval. A small classifier scores web content for prompt injection and warns the agent when a result looks hostile.

### Observability and visibility

Every verdict, change and reversal is a row in the ledger, a JSONL file in git, so the history reads as diffs. A `view` command prints the state of the loop: open strikes, fixes waiting for verification, strikes per layer and my complaints per week. After each judge run, the agent gets one line with the counts, the time window and the top strike, which I read in the pane I already watch; the ids stay out of it, because the worker reads them from the ledger. A failed judge run raises a critical desktop notice, and a held lock makes a run exit without judging, so two runs never judge the same slice.

## Conclusion

The model under my agent did not change during any of this. The harness did, and two ideas made those changes count. A fix lives in the harness as a mechanism: a rule in a skill file asks the model to remember, while a hook runs whether the model remembers or not. And an improvement is a result observed by someone other than the agent that made the change.

One question stays open: does the agent find its own deficiencies, or does an external judge find them while the agent experiments with the fixes? For now, mine is the second kind. The judge finds the problems, and the agent decides what to change, makes the change and tests it.

Benefits:

- My complaints become strikes on their own; I don't type verdicts or keep a score
- Changes live in hooks, scripts and tools, so they survive `/clear`, compaction and new sessions
- Every change comes with a regression test built from the real conversation
- A change counts as verified only when the mistake stops showing up, and a repeat triggers the next fix instead of a shrug
- The loop improves its own machinery through the same path, from judge failures to blind spots in the slicer
- The ledger is plain JSONL in git, so every verdict, change and reversal is a diff I can read

Challenges:

- Two days of data prove little. The complaints-per-week anchor has exactly one week, so there is no trend yet
- The judge is a frontier model, and it is not cheap. One slice of 142 KB took 735 seconds, and rerunning the frozen fixture after a rubric change is estimated at about 250k input tokens
- The judge can be wrong. Silent strikes wait for a repeat for that reason, and 33 verdicts were replaced after a corrected rerun
- The loop can overreach. When long task titles were cut off in my terminal, the agent offered a hook that would block any title over 60 characters; I said "it's fine if they are longer", and that offer became a strike of its own
- The plumbing breaks like any other plumbing. For about twelve hours, the second session held the notification stream and logged the main session's records as receipts, so the reflect step never ran. Records now go to the main session as direct messages
- Some behaviour has no mechanism. Those strikes stay open, and 78 are open right now
- A fix can pass its own test and still fail. 31 fixes have already seen their family come back, and the most frequent family, the agent's empty all-clear replies, still repeats. Harness research finds the same: "search-set gains often fail to transfer to held-out tasks" ([Harness-Delta Attribution](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22466))
- "Verified" means the judge has not seen the mistake for at least a day after the change. The judge reads a slice of each conversation, and it can miss things

The most useful outcome so far is that "my agent keeps doing X" stopped being a feeling and became a family in a ledger, with a list of changes that were tried and a record of whether each one held.

If you build something similar for your own agent, start with the separation: a judge that quotes its evidence and cannot rewrite its own rubric, and a rule that a change only counts when a later run agrees.
